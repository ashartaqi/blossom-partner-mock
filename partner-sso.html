Partner SSO · OpenID Connect · Authorization Code + PKCE

One tap on Investments, and they are already signed in.

A member of Blossom taps Investments and lands in the Whitelabel investing app with an account, a session and their name already filled in: no second signup, no password, nothing typed. This is how, what each side has to build, and what happens in every case where it does not go to plan.

01The shape of it

Five hops. Two of them are visible to the member as a flicker of the address bar; one of them is the only place their identity actually moves, and their browser is not part of it.

02Two channels, and why the distinction is the whole design

Every value in this system belongs to one of two channels, and which channel it travels on decides how much it is trusted.

Front channelBack channel
Who carries it The member's browser, via redirects Surmount's server, calling Blossom's directly
Who can read it The member. Anything on their machine. Browser history, Referer headers, proxy logs Nobody outside the TLS connection
Who can forge it Anyone. A URL is just a string Only a caller holding the client secret
What we put on it Random handles, valid once, dead in a minute The identity itself, signed
What we believe nothing until it is checked against something the browser could not touch the signature, and only after six separate checks pass
The rule that follows from it

Anything an attacker can put in a URL, we check against something they cannot: state against a value in an HttpOnly cookie, nonce against a claim inside a signed token, the authorization code against a PKCE verifier that never left our server.

03Why a signed assertion, and not a shared secret

This is the difference between OpenID Connect and the two-endpoint hand-off it replaced, and it is worth one diagram.

04First visit, in full

A member who has never opened the investing app. Every URL and payload below is real, copied from a working run, with secrets shortened.

Why the JWT is not simply in the redirect

URLs are not private. They land in browser history, in the Referer header of the next outbound request, in proxy logs, in access logs, and in whatever the member pastes into a chat window. A JWT in a URL is a live credential in all of those places for as long as it lives. A 60-second single-use handle is worth nothing by the time anyone reads it out of a log.

05Which account the member lands in

One of three things is true when a member arrives, and the sub in their token is enough to tell which. Every branch ends with a signed-in member. None of them asks the member to fill anything in.

The three outcomes

What we findWhat we doWhat the member sees
A link already exists for this sub Sign them in Their portfolio
No link, but a Surmount account on this email Adopt that account and write the link Their existing portfolio, intact
Neither Create the account and write the link A new, empty portfolio

Rows two and three run at most once per member, ever. Writing the link is what turns every later arrival into row one: a single indexed lookup, no decision, no form.

A fourth outcome stops the flow rather than completing it: if either lookup finds a closed account, we refuse instead of quietly reopening it. Section 8.1 covers why that is a compliance decision rather than a UX one.

Why row one matches on sub and never on email

sub is the partner's own user ID, their database primary key, sent to us as OpenID Connect's subject identifier. It carries two hard requirements that the whole design rests on:

  • It never changes for a given person.
  • It is never reused for a different one.

A UUID primary key satisfies both for free. An email address satisfies neither: people change them, and companies recycle them. Matching a recycled email would hand one person another person's brokerage account, which is not a bug you get to fix afterwards.

One claim Blossom has to be honest about

If email_verified is true we take Blossom's word for it, skip our own verification email, and match that address against an existing Surmount account on row two above. The second consequence is the sharp one: if their platform lets someone set an address without proving they own it, row two becomes a route into another member's brokerage account. Send false and we will verify it ourselves.

06Normal life

The cases that are not the first visit, and are not anyone attacking anything.

6.1

The member comes back

signed in

Everything up to the account lookup is identical. The lookup finds the link on the first branch and returns immediately: no creation, no form, no round trip to /userinfo that changes anything.

6.2

Not signed into Blossom right now

signed in, after one password

A bookmark, a new device, an expired session. The authorize endpoint has no session cookie to work with, so Blossom shows Blossom's own login page and carries the original request in ?next=. Signing in continues the hand-off rather than dumping them on a dashboard.

6.3

They already had a Surmount account

adopted

Somebody who signed up on the Whitelabel directly, before the integration existed. Creating a second account would collide on (email, organization) and on the generated username, so the right move is to take over the one already there and write the link.

6.4

Signing out

back to Blossom

An SSO account's password is 32 random bytes that nobody has ever seen. A sign-in form is therefore a wall the member cannot get past, so every exit from the Whitelabel leads back to Blossom, never to /signin.

07Under attack

Each of these is a real, named attack against OAuth deployments. Each one is stopped by one specific mechanism, and every one is covered by a test.

7.1

Login CSRF: a callback from somebody else's session

refused before any network call

The attacker starts a hand-off in their own browser, captures the callback URL with their code in it, and gets the victim to open it. If it worked, the victim would end up signed into the attacker's investing account and start typing real details into it.

7.2

A stolen authorization code

useless without the verifier

Codes leak: through a malicious browser extension, a shared machine, a mis-scoped referrer, an OS-level URL handler on mobile. PKCE assumes they will.

7.3

A forged or replayed ID token

signature and nonce
7.4

An attacker-supplied redirect URI

exact match, never redirected to

The most reliably exploited bug in OAuth deployments. Anything looser than exact matching (prefix, wildcard, subdomain) lets an attacker append their own host and receive the code.

7.5

A replayed callback, from browser history

single use, on both sides

08When things break

Failures that are nobody's fault. What the member sees, and whether trying again will help.

8.1

The account was closed

no retry offered

A closed brokerage account has a closed custodian account and retained records behind it. Silently creating a replacement would reopen it: a compliance problem, not a UX one. Both lookups check for this; without the check, the closed row would fall through to the adopt branch, we would issue a token, and the member would appear to sign in and then hit a hard failure on their first request.

8.2

The member cancelled, or Blossom refused

retry offered

Blossom reports refusals in the redirect rather than by failing it: a member who pressed Cancel on a consent screen, a session that could not be established, a client that has been suspended. We treat all of them as one thing.

What arrives at our callback
GET /api/sso/blossom/callback/?error=access_denied
                                 &error_description=User+denied+access
                                 &state=s-hp-ADERCXb5…

→ 302 https://staging.d2f7hqwlcsxwun.amplifyapp.com/sso/callback?error=invalid_code

Note what we do not pass on: error_description. It is attacker-controllable text arriving in a URL, and reflecting it would make our error page a mirror. It goes to the audit log instead.

8.3

The session code was already spent

retry offered

React runs effects twice in development, and members double-click. The redemption is guarded on the frontend, and the code is deleted server-side on read, so a second attempt gets a clean 400 and the member gets a retry rather than a spinner that never resolves.

8.4

Blossom is unreachable

retry offered

Discovery, JWKS, token and userinfo calls all have a ten-second timeout. Discovery and JWKS are cached for an hour, so a brief outage at Blossom does not stop members who are mid-flow. A failure at the token endpoint produces ?error=invalid_code with a Try again button.

One deliberate exception: if /userinfo is down, the sign-in still succeeds. The ID token already established who this is; userinfo only ever fills in decoration, and a missing avatar should never be the reason somebody cannot invest.

Every error slug, and what the member sees

SlugCauseMember seesRetry
flow_expired No cookie, expired flow, wrong state, replayed callback “That took too long” yes
invalid_code Code expired or spent, provider refused, ID token failed verification “We couldn't sign you in” yes
account_closed Their Surmount account is closed “Account closed. Contact support” no
sso_unavailable Unknown partner, missing organization: our misconfiguration “Investing is unavailable” no
unsupported /start/ on a partner that can only initiate from their side “Start from your dashboard” no
Why the slugs are so coarse

A callback that reported why it failed would be an oracle: an attacker could tell “already used” from “never existed” and learn which codes were real. Five slugs from a closed set go in the URL; the actual reason goes to the audit log, where the person who needs it is.

And no failure ever leads to a sign-in page. These accounts have a random password nobody knows, so a password form is a wall the member cannot get past. Every dead end leads back to Blossom.

09Edge cases worth knowing about

SituationWhat happens
Member changes their email on Blossom Nothing breaks. We match on sub, which did not change. The new address is picked up on the next hand-off.
Two people, same email, different Blossom accounts Two different sub values, so two separate links. The second one hits the unique constraint on (email, organization) and fails loudly rather than merging two humans.
Member deleted on Blossom's side They can no longer sign into Blossom, so they never reach us. Their Surmount account is untouched and stays closable through our own flow.
Member suspended between the two legs The code was minted while they were active. We check again at redemption rather than trusting the snapshot, so no token is issued.
Blossom rotates its signing key The cached JWKS misses the new kid, so we refetch once and continue. No downtime, no coordination. Only a cached set that has already failed triggers a refetch, so a junk token cannot force one.
Blossom rotates the client secret A coordinated change, both sides at once. Unlike the signing key, which is why the secret should be the less-used credential.
Two tabs, two hand-offs at once The second /start/ overwrites the flow cookie, so the first tab's callback finds a mismatched state and is refused. The member retries in that tab. Correct, if slightly annoying.
Member's clock is wrong Irrelevant. Every expiry check happens on a server; the browser's clock is never consulted.
Server clocks disagree 60 seconds of leeway on exp and iat. Enough for real NTP drift, not enough to usefully extend an expired token.
Blossom sends a picture we won't render Dropped, and the sign-in continues. Only https is accepted. A javascript: or data: URL reaching an <img src> is how a partner-supplied string becomes script execution on our origin.
Blossom's ID token is minimal Perfectly fine. We fetch the rest from /userinfo and check that its sub matches the token's. If they disagree, we take neither.
Member blocks cookies The flow cookie cannot be set, so the callback is refused with flow_expired. This is correct: without it there is no CSRF defence, and proceeding anyway would be worse than failing.
Member wants a specific page ?next=/portfolio on the start URL. Accepted only as a path on our own app. An absolute URL is dropped, because an open redirect that fires after a successful sign-in is the worst kind.
A second partner arrives An entry in SSO_PARTNERS. No route, no view, no branch. The partner is a URL segment and the adapter is chosen by configuration.

10What Surmount needs from Blossom's team

All of it is standard OpenID Connect. If Blossom already runs an identity provider (for staff SSO, for a mobile app, for anything) most of this is already done, and the work is registering one more client.

The whole thing in one line

You send us one checklist. We give you two URLs. You add a link. There is no SDK to install and no API for your frontend to call. The checklist is seven items long and every one of them is something you already have or can create in an afternoon.

Seven questions, before anybody writes code

Answering these takes ten minutes and tells both sides immediately whether this is a two-day integration or a two-week one. If the answer to any of them is no, that is useful too; every one has a documented path forward.

What you should have in place before starting

None of this is specific to Surmount. If Blossom already runs an identity provider for anything, all six are already true and the work is registering one more client.

  • An OpenID Provider reachable over https with a valid certificate, on a hostname you control and do not plan to move
  • An RSA signing keypair (2048-bit or better) with a stable kid, and somewhere safe to keep the private half
  • A client registry you can add an entry to, storing a client ID, a secret, and an exact-match list of redirect URIs
  • Your existing user model and session wired to the authorize endpoint, so a member with a live Blossom session is not asked to log in again
  • A sandbox environment that is separate from production, with its own keys, its own client registrations, and its own test data
  • Somewhere to read logs from the authorize and token endpoints. Most of the integration debugging happens there, not on our side
1

Your issuer URL

One value: whatever hostname your OpenID Provider runs on, for example https://id.blossom.com. Everything else we need (the authorize, token and userinfo endpoints, your signing keys, the algorithms you support) we read from {issuer}/.well-known/openid-configuration. Those URLs stay yours to change without telling us.

Two things it has to satisfy. It must be https in production, and it must match the iss claim in your ID tokens byte for byte, including the trailing slash or its absence. That mismatch is the most common way this integration fails on the first attempt, and the error it produces, a token that verifies but is rejected, does not point at the cause.

2

A client ID and secret

Register Surmount as a confidential client and send us both values. We need them before we can call your token endpoint at all.

The client ID is not a secret. It travels in the authorize URL and is visible in the member's address bar. The secret is, and it only ever moves server to server. Client IDs have no required format; a readable slug is easier to trace in your logs than a random string. The examples on this page use surmount-investing. If your provider generates its own, send us whatever it generates and we will use that.

Mark the client trusted, so it skips the consent screen. Surmount is first-party from the member's point of view, and a prompt asking whether Blossom may share their name with Blossom is noise.

3

Allow-list our callback

Register this as the redirect URI on that client. This is our staging backend, which is what we will be testing against. The production URL follows the same shape and will be sent before go-live.

https://staging.surmount.ai/api/sso/blossom/callback/

Exact match. No wildcards, no prefix matching, no subdomain matching. Loose redirect-URI matching is the single most exploited bug in OAuth deployments. The trailing slash is part of the value.

Worth registering too, so signing out of the investing app returns members to you: your own dashboard URL, as a post-logout redirect.

4

Add the link

<a href="https://staging.surmount.ai/api/sso/blossom/start/">Investments</a>

A plain anchor, not fetch, not XMLHttpRequest, not a popup. The hand-off is a chain of redirects that has to carry cookies across origins, and only a top-level navigation does that. Nothing happens at click time and your frontend holds nothing.

Do not bridge the two

Three names, and they are not interchangeable

blossom is the partner slug, a URL segment we own. It is baked into the redirect URI, so once you have allow-listed it, it is fixed. surmount-investing is the client ID, which lives in your registry and reappears in the aud claim. Your issuer is a third thing again. All three are compared by exact string match somewhere in the flow, so if any of them changes after registration, sign-in stops until both sides update together.

The endpoints we call, and what we do with each

Five endpoints. We discover four of them from the fifth, so the only URL you have to tell us is the issuer.

EndpointDiscovery keyMethodHow we authenticateWhat it is for
Discovery (the well-known path) GET none, it is public Read the four below, plus your supported algorithms. Cached for an hour
Authorization authorization_endpoint GET the member's own Blossom session Where we send the member's browser. You authenticate them and redirect back with a code
Token token_endpoint POST client_secret_basic Exchange the code plus our PKCE verifier for an ID token. Server to server, never the browser
JWKS jwks_uri GET none, it is public Fetch your public signing keys so we can verify the ID token. Cached for an hour
UserInfo optional userinfo_endpoint GET Bearer, using the access token you just issued Fill in claims the ID token left out. If it fails, sign-in still succeeds

We do not call revocation, introspection, registration, or end-session. If your discovery document advertises them that is fine; we simply do not use them.

Where the discovery document comes from

You almost certainly do not have to write /.well-known/openid-configuration by hand. node-oidc-provider, Spring Authorization Server, Duende IdentityServer and OpenIddict all publish it automatically, generated from the client and key configuration you have already done.

Two exceptions worth knowing about before you scope this. With authlib (Python) and fosite (Go) you assemble the document yourself, but it is a static dictionary of roughly twenty lines, not logic. The reference provider described in section 11 does exactly that, and it is the shortest file on which to copy the shape.

However it is produced, one rule: the issuer field inside the document, the URL it is served from, and the iss claim in your tokens must all be the same string.

Are these URLs meant to be public?

Yes, both of them, and it is not a compromise. Partners ask this early, so it is worth answering plainly.

The callback has no choice. It is the redirect_uri, so the member's browser has to be able to navigate to it. It appears in the address bar, in browser history, and in the client registration on your side. There is no version of OAuth in which it is secret.

The start URL is public by design. It is an anchor on a dashboard. Someone who opens it without a Blossom session is redirected to your login page, which is the correct outcome, and leaks nothing about anybody.

None of the security in this design rests on a URL being hard to guess. It rests on state bound to an HttpOnly cookie, a PKCE verifier that never leaves our server, and a client secret that only ever moves between servers. Every front-channel endpoint in every OAuth deployment is public; secrecy lives on the back channel, which is the whole point of section 2.

The claims we read

ClaimWhat we do with it
subrequiredThe permanent link between your member and their Surmount account
emailrequiredCreating the account; matching an existing one
namerequiredTheir name in the app. One field, whatever you display today
email_verifiedpreferredIf true, we skip our own verification email
given_name, family_nameoptionalOnly if you already store them separately. We never ask you to split a name you hold as one field
pictureoptionalTheir avatar. Must be an https URL

If your ID token is minimal, that is fine. Anything missing we fetch from /userinfo straight afterwards. And if /userinfo happens to be down, sign-in still succeeds on what the ID token already established.

On the name specifically: we need one display name, not a structured one. Where our KYC flow wants a first and last name it derives them and asks the member to confirm, so a split we guessed wrong costs nothing, and you never have to build a splitter for a name your system stores whole.

sub: your user ID, and the one value that must never move

Send whatever your database already uses as a primary key: a UUID, an integer, a ULID. We treat it as opaque and never parse it or display it.

  • It never changes for a given person.
  • It is never reused for a different one.

A primary key satisfies both for free. Do not send an email address or a username. People change them and companies recycle them, and matching a recycled address would hand one person another person's brokerage account, not a bug anyone gets to fix afterwards.

Decide public or pairwise before launch, not after

If your provider supports pairwise subject identifiers (a different sub per client, advertised as subject_types_supported in the discovery document) choose now. Either setting works for us. But switching it after go-live changes every sub we have stored, so every existing member arrives looking like somebody brand new, with no route back to their portfolio. It is the one setting in this integration with no migration path.

One claim we cannot check for ourselves

When email_verified is true we take your word for it and skip our own verification email. More consequentially, we will match that address against an existing Surmount account and hand it over. If your platform lets somebody set an address without proving they own it, that claim becomes a route into another member's account. Send false and we will verify it ourselves; it costs the member one email and costs you nothing.

Implementation checklist

In order. Each step is verifiable on its own, so a failure tells you which one broke rather than leaving you with a redirect loop and no information.

StepHow you know it worked
1 Stand up the provider in sandbox and generate a signing keypair GET {issuer}/.well-known/openid-configuration returns 200, and the issuer field inside it matches the URL you fetched it from
2 Publish the JWKS GET the jwks_uri from that document. It returns 200 with at least one RSA key carrying a kid
3 Wire the authorize endpoint to your existing session A signed-in member hitting it is redirected straight back with a code, without a second login prompt. A signed-out one sees your own login page
4 Register the client: ID, secret, and our callback as an exact-match redirect URI An authorize request with a URI that is not on the list is refused on your own page, and is not redirected anywhere
5 Require PKCE with S256 An authorize request with no code_challenge is rejected. One with code_challenge_method=plain is also rejected
6 Issue ID tokens with the claims in the table above Decode one. It carries sub, email, name, the nonce we sent, aud equal to our client ID, and iss byte-identical to the discovery document
7 Create the test accounts You can sign in as each of them on your sandbox
8 Send us the seven checklist values We confirm receipt and start walking the flow
9 Add the Investments link to your sandbox dashboard Clicking it lands a signed-in member in the investing app with their name already filled in
10 Repeat 1, 2, 4 and 9 against production Same checks, production values, before the cutover rather than on the day
Integration is complete when all of these are true
  • A member already signed into Blossom taps Investments and reaches their portfolio with no second login and no form
  • A member who is signed out sees Blossom's own login page, and signing in there continues the hand-off rather than dropping them on a dashboard
  • The same member returning lands in the same Surmount account every time, including after they change their email address on Blossom
  • A member with a pre-existing Surmount account on the same email is signed into that account, with their portfolio and history intact, rather than a duplicate
  • An authorize request with an unregistered redirect URI is refused, and the refusal is not redirected anywhere
  • A replayed callback URL from browser history fails cleanly and offers a retry, rather than signing anybody in
  • A suspended or disabled member is refused at redemption, not signed in on a stale code
  • Every failure in section 8 shows the member a readable message, and the real reason is in the logs rather than the URL
  • Both sides have walked all of the above in sandbox, and steps 1, 2, 4 and 9 have been repeated in production

What you do not need to build

  • Account creation on our side: first arrival creates it, silently
  • Linking a member who already has a Surmount account: we adopt it
  • A signup form, a password, or an email confirmation
  • Phone verification: our KYC flow collects and OTP-verifies it later
  • Retries, expiry handling, or error screens

11If Blossom has no OpenID Provider

Then the honest question is whether standing one up is worth it, and usually it is. You will not be writing the protocol yourself: every mainstream stack has a maintained OpenID Connect server you configure rather than code. Point it at the user model and session you already have, and register one client.

A working reference is available

We built a complete OpenID Provider in Python (authlib, about 400 lines, 33 tests) while developing this integration, and it is the mock partner platform every case on this page was walked against. It is a conformance target: run it locally, point a test client at it, and compare its behaviour with your own.

For production, use the server implementation for your stack directly rather than porting ours. It is a development tool, not a hardened provider, and the libraries above have had far more scrutiny than four hundred lines of ours ever will.

If it genuinely is not on the table

We keep a simpler two-endpoint fallback behind the same seam. It works, it is tested, and it has the same one-time-code shape. It is weaker in four specific ways, and the choice should be made with them in view:

OpenID ConnectToken exchange
Trust model asymmetric we hold only a public key symmetric both sides can mint what the other verifies
The assertion Signed. Re-verifiable later, showable to an auditor Nothing is signed. Trust rests on TLS and the secret
Who can start it Either side. A “Continue with Blossom” button works too Only Blossom. They mint the first code
Rotating credentials Publish the new key beside the old one; clients follow A coordinated change on both sides
Who wrote the security code Authlib, audited, widely deployed Us, and them

What it keeps: one-time codes that expire in seconds, an exact-match redirect allow-list, constant-time credential comparison, and identity that only ever moves server to server. Those properties matter most and do not depend on the protocol.

Known, and deliberately left

Flagged, not changed

ACCESS_TOKEN_LIFETIME_MINUTES defaults to 86400, sixty days, and the local environment sets it to roughly two years. That is a platform-wide default rather than an SSO one, so changing it here would alter behaviour for every user of the backend, not just partner members. It is worth fixing before this ships, especially since a member's Blossom session may last an hour.

Two things this work deliberately does not do: it does not implement OIDC back-channel logout (Blossom ending a session does not yet end ours; the standard supports it, and it is the natural next step), and it does not add a second partner, because the seam is only proven when there is one.

13Sample payloads, and what a healthy call looks like

The authorization request, the token request and the token response are in section 4, on the wire, with real values. The four below are the ones section 4 does not show, because the member's browser never sees them.

The discovery document

Fetched once an hour from {issuer}/.well-known/openid-configuration. Anything not listed here we ignore, so a longer document is fine. 

{
  "issuer": "https://id.blossom.com",
  "authorization_endpoint": "https://id.blossom.com/oauth/authorize",
  "token_endpoint": "https://id.blossom.com/oauth/token",
  "userinfo_endpoint": "https://id.blossom.com/oauth/userinfo",
  "jwks_uri": "https://id.blossom.com/.well-known/jwks.json",

  "response_types_supported": ["code"],
  "grant_types_supported": ["authorization_code"],
  "subject_types_supported": ["public"],
  "id_token_signing_alg_values_supported": ["RS256"],
  "code_challenge_methods_supported": ["S256"],
  "token_endpoint_auth_methods_supported": ["client_secret_basic"],
  "scopes_supported": ["openid", "profile", "email"],
  "claims_supported": [
    "sub", "iss", "aud", "exp", "iat", "auth_time", "nonce",
    "name", "email", "email_verified", "picture"
  ]
}
The one field that breaks integrations

issuer here, the URL this document is served from, and the iss claim in every ID token must be the same string. Not equivalent URLs: the same string. https://id.blossom.com and https://id.blossom.com/ are different, and the failure they produce is a token that verifies cryptographically and is then rejected, which points at nothing.

The JWKS

Fetched from jwks_uri, cached for an hour. During a key rotation it holds two keys and that is correct; see section 14. 

{
  "keys": [
    {
      "kty": "RSA",
      "use": "sig",
      "alg": "RS256",
      "kid": "G72wYKqu7QsLbYqAyGdN0kZ3xTfWnJ9m",
      "n": "0vx7agoebGcQSuuPiLJXZptN9nndrQmbXEps2aiAFbWhM78LhWx4…",
      "e": "AQAB"
    }
  ]
}

Only the public half. The private key never leaves Blossom, which is the whole reason this is an asymmetric design rather than a shared secret, and why section 3 spends a diagram on it.

The UserInfo response

Called with the access token from the token response. Optional: if you put every claim in the ID token we never come here at all. 

GET https://id.blossom.com/oauth/userinfo
Authorization: Bearer vLQ1yZ0…

→ 200 OK
{
  "sub": "8c14b7e2-0000-4000-8000-9d3e…",   ← must equal the ID token's sub
  "name": "Nadia Halim",
  "email": "nadia@blossom.test",
  "email_verified": true,
  "picture": "https://id.blossom.com/avatar/8c14b7e2-….svg"
}

If that sub disagrees with the one in the ID token we discard both and fail the sign-in. A mismatch means one of the two responses is not about the person we started the flow for, and there is no safe way to guess which.

What your provider should do when something is wrong

These are the behaviours we rely on. Most implementations get them right by default, but they are worth confirming in sandbox rather than discovering in production.

SituationCorrect behaviourWhy it matters
Redirect URI is not on the registered list Refuse on your own page. Do not redirect, not even to report the error A redirected error still delivers the browser to the attacker's host, wearing Blossom's name. Section 7.4
Authorization code presented twice Refuse the second, and revoke any tokens already issued against it The revocation is what makes a leaked code worthless rather than merely raced
code_verifier missing or wrong 400 invalid_grant This is the check that makes a stolen code useless. Section 7.2
code_challenge absent on the authorize request Refuse the request We always send it. If you accept requests without it, someone else's client will omit it
code_challenge_method=plain Refuse plain is the verifier in clear text, so it protects nothing

Client secret is wrong 401 invalid_client, with no detail about which part was wrong Detailed credential errors are an oracle. Section 8 explains the same reasoning on our side

Rotating the signing key

No coordination with us, and no downtime, as long as the new key is published before it is used. This is the asymmetry from section 3 paying for itself.

  1. Generate the new keypair with a new kid. Never reuse one
  2. Publish the public half in your JWKS alongside the old key. Both are now served
  3. Wait at least one hour, so every cached copy of your JWKS has expired and been refetched
  4. Start signing new ID tokens with the new kid
  5. Keep the old key published for at least 24 hours after the last token signed with it has expired
  6. Remove the old key

If you skip step 3 we still recover: a kid we do not recognise triggers one refetch, and the sign-in continues. But that is a fallback, not the plan, and it turns a rotation into a burst of traffic at your JWKS endpoint.

Rotating the client secret

This one does need both sides, which is the honest argument for keeping the secret the less-used credential.

  1. Create a second active secret on the same client. Most implementations allow two
  2. Send us the new value. We deploy it; both secrets work during this window
  3. Confirm in your token endpoint logs that our traffic is arriving on the new one
  4. Revoke the old secret
If your provider only allows one secret at a time

Then there is no overlap window and no way to make the change invisible. Pick a low traffic hour, change both sides together, and expect a short period where sign-ins fail with a retry. Members who retry after the change succeed. Nobody loses an account, and nothing needs re-linking, but it is a scheduled interruption rather than a silent one.

15When it does not work, in order of likelihood

Ten failures, drawn from building this against a provider from scratch. The first four account for most first-day problems, and none of them are subtle once you know where to look.

SymptomAlmost alwaysFix
1 Signature verifies, token still rejected iss does not match your discovery document byte for byte. Usually a trailing slash Make the issuer field, the URL it is served from, and the iss claim one identical string
2 redirect_uri mismatch on the authorize request Trailing slash, http against https, or a percent-encoding difference Copy the URI from section 10 rather than retyping it. Exact match means exact
3 401 invalid_client at the token endpoint Wrong secret, or the wrong authentication method. We send HTTP Basic Confirm client_secret_basic is enabled, and that you sent us the sandbox secret rather than the production one
4 Works in sandbox, fails in production The production client was never registered, or our production callback is not on its allow-list Repeat steps 1, 2, 4 and 9 of the checklist against production. Two environments, two registrations
5 Member sees “That took too long” Our flow cookie is missing: either the member blocks cookies, or more than ten minutes passed at your login screen Nothing on your side. Confirm your login page carries the original request in ?next= so members are not detoured
6 Member sees “We couldn't sign you in” Token exchange failed, or the ID token failed one of the six checks in section 4 Your token endpoint log has the real reason. Ours is deliberately coarse in the URL and specific in the audit log
7 nonce mismatch The provider is not echoing the nonce from the authorize request into the ID token Enable it. Without it there is no replay defence, and we will refuse every token
8 aud mismatch The provider is putting a client name in aud instead of the client ID aud must be the client ID exactly. A string or a single-element array are both fine
9 Everything breaks right after a key rotation The new key was used before it was published, or it reused an old kid Section 14. Publish, wait an hour, then sign
10 A member lands in the wrong account, or a brand new one sub changed for that person. A migration, an IdP swap, or a switch between public and pairwise subjects Stop and tell us before more members arrive. sub is the permanent link and there is no automatic repair
Where to look first

Your authorize and token endpoint logs hold the real reason for almost everything above. Our callback deliberately reports a coarse error slug in the URL, because a callback that explains precisely why it failed is an oracle. The specific reason goes to our audit log, and we will read it out on request.
